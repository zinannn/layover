Where the dead hours come from

01 — The problem

Commuter students at RIT Dubai don't have free time. They have stranded time.

RIT Dubai sits in Dubai Silicon Oasis, and most of its students do not live there. They arrive on the university’s commuter buses from Al Qusais, Mirdif, Deira, Al Barsha, Sharjah, Ajman and Abu Dhabi.

Here is the part almost nobody designs around. The commuter bus has one arrival in the morning and two departures in the afternoon — 3:00 p.m. and 6:00 p.m., from Parking Lot 3. Not "every twenty minutes". Two.

So a student whose last class ends at 3:10 p.m. does not leave at 3:10 p.m. They leave at 6:00. A student whose first class is at 11:00 a.m. did not arrive at 11:00 — they arrived on the single morning bus. The timetable hands them hours they never chose, at both ends of the day, every week, for four years.

That time is not rest and it is not study. It is the worst kind of time: too long to waste, too short to go home, and almost always spent alone — because no student can see who else is stranded in the same window.

Without a permanent designated area to call their own, commuter students are left to wander between classes feeling like temporary visitors rather than integral members of the campus community. UIC Commuter Student Sense of Belonging Report

—
The consequence is documented, not local. Commuter students report a lower sense of institutional belonging, spend less time on campus outside class, and have less access to informal peer support and societies. Researchers record the same phrase from students repeatedly: having “hours to kill in between classes” with nowhere to be. (UIC; QMUL; NCWIT)

Who is actually affected

  • Bus-pass commuters — the sharpest case. Their departure time is fixed by a contract, not a choice, and they cannot leave early even when nothing is left in their day.
  • Second-year students on mandatory co-op. Co-op is compulsory at RIT Dubai, and the preparation that decides who lands a placement — CV review, mock interviews, practice — competes directly with the exact hours these students spend sitting in a corridor.
  • First-year and transfer students, who have not yet built the informal WhatsApp network that is currently the only way to discover that someone else is free at the same time.
  • Students who want company without conversation — a real and under-served need. Existing social tools assume everyone wants to talk.
  • Students living in DSO housing are the control group: a ten-minute walk home means their dead time is optional. Same campus, entirely different problem.

03 — The solution

Layover treats the bus timetable as the social graph.

Every other campus platform matches students on interests. On a commuter campus, interest is not the binding constraint — co-presence is. Layover computes the one window in which a meeting is physically possible, and builds everything on top of that.

Reads the real timetable

You enter your classes and pick your actual RIT route. Layover derives your true campus day — morning arrival to bus departure — and isolates three kinds of dead time: the head (waiting for your first class), the middle (gaps between classes) and the tail (last class to the 15:00 or 18:00 bus).

The Departure Board

A live board of everyone currently grounded, ordered the way a terminal orders flights. Each row shows how long they're free, where they are, which bus they're on, and the exact overlap in minutes with your own window. If it's under twenty minutes, it says so instead of pretending it's a match.

Gates

Plans sized to a layover, not events you must leave early: a 90-minute block for a course you share, a co-op mock interview before a careers fair, a gym slot, a food run, and a Quiet Gate for students who want company without conversation. Each one is checked against your window and your bus before you can join.

Reroute

The part that prevents the problem instead of decorating it. Before registration, test a section swap and see what it does to the shape of the week — campus days, dead hours, and the share of your bus-pass fee spent on hours you didn't choose. "This swap removes 4.5 dead hours a week and one whole campus day."

People you can actually find

An account puts you on the board for other students and keeps your timetable across devices. The People page lists everyone on Layover with search by name or major, sort by name, major, year or bus route, and filters for route, year, shared courses, the same bus, and quiet company — so adding the right person never means scrolling the whole campus. Friend requests and the board filter for friends build on top of that.

Designed for different needs

  • Quiet Gate is a first-class feature, not an afterthought: co-presence with an explicit no-conversation norm, for students who find unstructured social time costly rather than restorative.
  • Every gate publishes its exact location and window up front, so a student with a mobility need or limited energy can judge the walk before committing, rather than discovering it on arrival.
  • The board is a real HTML table with proper headers, works entirely by keyboard, and every status is carried by text as well as colour — nothing depends on distinguishing amber from green.
  • Colour follows RIT’s published brand rule rather than approximating it: RIT Orange #F76902 is used only for fills and type at 18.76px bold or larger, and RIT’s accessible orange #C75300 carries everything smaller.
  • Both a light and a dark theme are designed, and all motion is disabled automatically under prefers-reduced-motion.
  • No account is needed to explore: the board, gates and Reroute come with a built-in week. Sign in only when you want to be seen — your timetable syncs across devices, you appear on the board for others while you opt to stay visible, and nothing else leaves your device.

04 — Why it matters

The hours are already paid for. Right now they're just lost.

A student on the Ajman route pays AED 9,900 a year for a seat on a bus that leaves at 3:00 and 6:00. Those campus hours are not optional; they will be spent either way. The only open question is whether they are spent sitting in a corridor or doing something the student chose.

The students who lose this time are the same students the research finds with weaker peer networks and less access to informal support — the support that decides who hears about a co-op opening and who feels able to ask for help. At RIT Dubai, where co-op is compulsory, that is not only a social loss.

And the fix does not need a new building, a new budget line, or a change to the bus contract. The time already exists and the people are already on the same campus. The only missing piece is the one thing a timetable cannot tell you on its own: who else is here, and for how much longer.

Layover needs no new building, no new budget and no change to the bus contract. It starts working the moment two students’ dead hours overlap.
